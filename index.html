BRIDGING BARRIERS30-minute research presentation · University English teachers
Research presentation · 30 minutes

Bridging Barriers

Aural cues, codeswitching, and proficiency in vocabulary learning for visually impaired learners

Areen Badri, Suzanne Graham and Pengchong Zhang
Studies in Second Language Acquisition (2026), 48, 118–139
Cambridge University Press · DOI: 10.1017/S0272263125101101

Central question: Can making target words more noticeable in spoken input, while explaining them in learners’ first language, improve L2 vocabulary learning?

Speaker notes · 1 minute

Introduce the article and explain that the presentation focuses on what the researchers investigated, how they investigated it, what they found, and what university English teachers might take from it. The participants were Saudi female high-school EFL learners, not university students. The audience for this presentation is university English teachers.

01 · Rationale

Why investigate vocabulary learning for VI learners?

2.2bn

People globally were estimated to have some degree of visual impairment (WHO, 2019, as cited in the article).

Research gap

The authors identify a shortage of rigorous class-level empirical research on effective foreign-language teaching approaches for VI learners.

Access to input

Language lessons often rely heavily on visually presented material, which can create barriers in inclusive classrooms.

Not simply an advantage

Listening may be a primary information channel, but possible challenges in concept formation and L1–L2 mapping also matter.

Speaker notes · 2 minutes

The article argues that VI learners need strong vocabulary knowledge just as sighted learners do, but there has been limited evidence about which instructional approaches work for them. Avoid assuming that stronger auditory abilities automatically mean strong L2 listening proficiency. The article treats VI learners as a diverse group and notes that the nature and extent of concept-formation difficulties remain debated.

02 · Theoretical foundations

Three ideas behind the intervention

Noticing

Schmidt (2012): consciously noticed input can become intake. Learners need to attend to target forms, not only overall meaning.

Input enhancement

Sharwood Smith (1993): input is manipulated to make selected linguistic features more noticeable.

L1 lemma mediation

Jiang (2000): an L2 word may connect to a conceptual representation through its L1 equivalent during vocabulary development.

Teaching logic: make the spoken English word salient, then support its meaning through an L1 explanation within contextualized L2 input.

Speaker notes · 2 minutes

Briefly explain the three-stage model from Jiang: the formal stage, the L1 lemma mediation stage, and the L2 integration stage. The article suggests that L1 explanations may help connect an L2 word to an existing conceptual representation. It also cautions that learners need contextualized L2 input; otherwise they may become over-reliant on translation and fail to develop fuller word knowledge. This intervention is an example of lexical focus-on-form: attention is drawn to a linguistic item within a meaning-focused activity.

In this experiment, the AIM manipulation specifically involved increased volume of target items, followed by CS explanations.

Speaker notes · 2 minutes

Be precise: AIM is the broader term, but the tested manipulation in this study was increased volume. Do not imply that the researchers tested every possible AIM technique. CS alone was the comparison condition; AIM alone was not tested.

04 · Research questions

What did the researchers want to find out?

RQ1 · Instructional effect

What is the effect of two types of vocabulary instruction (CS and AIMCS—artificially increasing the volume followed by CS) during aural activities on (a) VI and (b) SI learners?

RQ2 · Proficiency moderation

How is the instruction effect on vocabulary learning moderated by learners’ listening proficiency and their existing vocabulary size?

Speaker notes · 1.5 minutes

Explain that the researchers were not simply asking whether one technique works. They also examined whether outcomes differed for visually impaired and sighted learners, and whether listening proficiency or pre-existing vocabulary knowledge changed the effects. Their design compared CS with AIMCS to identify the added value of AIM over CS.

05 · Methodology

Who took part?

32

Total participants

16 + 16

16 VI and 16 sighted (SI) learners

16–20

Age range in years

  • Saudi female high-school EFL learners; Arabic was their L1.
  • Six years of English schooling; low–intermediate proficiency (IELTS 4.5–5).
  • Convenience sampling: VI learners recruited from an institute for blind people and a high school with a dedicated VI class; SI learners from the same high school.

Speaker notes · 1.5 minutes

Emphasize the sample carefully. It consisted of 32 Saudi female high-school learners, not university students. Recruitment of participants with disabilities can be challenging, which is why the researchers used convenience sampling. The small, context-specific sample is relevant when considering how widely to generalize the findings.

06 · Study design

A counterbalanced mixed design

Baseline

Listening comprehension and vocabulary knowledge tests

→

6 sessions

50 minutes each, across two academic terms

→

Post-test

At the end of each session

→

2 weeks later

Delayed post-test

Counterbalancing: within each session, five target items received AIMCS and five received CS; the order was reversed in the next session, and so on.

The design yielded 5,400 valid observations across 60 taught target items.

Speaker notes · 2 minutes

The study was quasi-experimental and used both within- and between-subjects comparisons. Each learner encountered both instructional conditions. The order was counterbalanced to reduce the possibility that one method benefited simply from appearing earlier or later. Six sessions were delivered by the first author. A post-test followed each session, with a delayed post-test two weeks later.

07 · Teaching procedure

What happened in a 50-minute session?

  1. Prepare for meaning: learners received multiple-choice listening comprehension questions.
  2. First listening: they heard the passage once, answered questions and discussed its meaning in L2.
  3. Segmented replay: the passage was replayed in segments; the teacher paused at sentence ends to explain target vocabulary.
  4. Instructional condition: CS or AIMCS was applied to the target item; an additional example was provided.
  5. Revisit meaning: the teacher reviewed the passage meaning in L2, repeated target explanations, and played the recording a third time.

Speaker notes · 2 minutes

Explain that learners first focused on the overall meaning rather than being alerted to unfamiliar words. In the segmented replay, explanations were matched for length and delivery time across conditions. Both groups could access written target words: PowerPoint slides for SI learners and an Immersive Reader-enabled Word document for VI learners. The listening comprehension question sheet was also made accessible using Microsoft Forms and Immersive Reader.

08 · Materials and measures

What was taught and how was learning measured?

60 target items

31 nouns, 18 adjectives and 11 verbs, selected from modified listening passages and judged unlikely to be known.

Listening passages

Selected from British Council and Unlock 3 materials, then modified for topic, complexity, speech rate and length.

Pre-existing vocabulary

Listening Vocabulary Levels Test (LVLT), adapted for Arabic L1 speakers; target items were embedded to reduce pre-treatment alerting.

Learning tests

Six post-tests and six delayed post-tests; meaning-recognition format, with scores out of 60.

The listening comprehension pre-test used the first two sections of an IELTS practice test (20 items).

Speaker notes · 1.5 minutes

The vocabulary items came from K2, K3 and K4 frequency bands, the Academic Word List and off-list words. The LVLT was used to assess general vocabulary knowledge and pre-existing knowledge of target items. The delayed post-test took place two weeks after each session. The article reports high internal consistency for the listening test, LVLT, post-tests and delayed post-tests.

Important design point: the study compared AIMCS with CS. It did not include an AIM-only condition or a no-treatment control group.

Speaker notes · 1.5 minutes

Use a simple illustrative example, clearly label it as your own example rather than one from the article: “The word is enormous” spoken with greater volume, followed by an Arabic explanation such as “ضخم جداً”. The researchers also provided an additional example of the target item. Their explanations were controlled to be identical in length and time taken in both conditions.

10 · Results · RQ1

What did the study find?

Short term

AIMCS advantage

AIMCS produced significantly better short-term vocabulary learning than CS for both VI and SI learners.

2 weeks

No clear condition advantage

The superiority of AIMCS over CS was not maintained at the delayed post-test.

Group effect

VI learners benefited more overall than SI learners in the short term.

Delayed outcome

The short-term VI advantage over SI learners disappeared at the delayed post-test.

Speaker notes · 2 minutes

This is the central result. Do not say AIMCS produced better long-term retention: the advantage over CS disappeared at the two-week delayed post-test. Likewise, VI learners’ greater overall benefit was a short-term effect and was not sustained as a group advantage at delay. The paper reports statistical comparisons; this slide summarizes the direction of the findings rather than inventing effect sizes.

11 · Results · RQ2

Proficiency mattered

Listening proficiency

For short-term learning, higher listening proficiency was associated with greater gains from AIMCS than from CS.

VI and listening

Listening proficiency had larger positive moderation effects for VI learners than for SI learners, in both short- and longer-term analyses.

Existing vocabulary

Learners with lower pre-existing vocabulary knowledge made greater gains, regardless of group or instructional condition.

Longer-term pattern

For longer-term learning, learners with different listening proficiency levels appeared to benefit similarly from CS and AIMCS.

Speaker notes · 2 minutes

Explain “moderation” in accessible terms: the effect of an instructional method was not the same for all learners; it varied with proficiency. Higher listening proficiency especially strengthened the short-term benefit of AIMCS. Lower pre-existing vocabulary was associated with larger gains across both groups and both conditions. This does not mean that low listening proficiency makes listening instruction unsuitable; it means listening proficiency is an important factor to consider and develop.

12 · Discussion

How do the authors interpret the results?

  • Noticing: increased volume may have made target words more salient and directed attention to their form.
  • L1 mediation: CS may have helped connect the L2 word with its corresponding concept through the L1.
  • Sensory compensation: auditory strengths may help explain why VI learners responded particularly well to aural instruction in the short term.
  • Depth of processing: drawing attention to a word and giving an L1 explanation may not, by themselves, create the elaborative processing needed for durable retention.

The authors suggest further contextualized encounters and interactive, productive use of target words may support longer-term retention.

Speaker notes · 2 minutes

Present these as the authors’ interpretations, not as mechanisms directly proven by the experiment. The authors connect the short-term benefit to Schmidt’s Noticing Hypothesis and VanPatten’s input processing account. They interpret CS through Jiang’s L1 lemma mediation stage. Sensory compensation is offered as a possible explanation for VI learners’ stronger short-term response to auditory input. The article does not establish that all VI learners have superior listening proficiency.

13 · Limitations and future research

What remains unanswered?

  • No AIM-only condition: the study cannot isolate the contribution of AIM from the contribution of CS.
  • No traditional-instruction comparison: future work should compare AIMCS with more conventional L2 teaching, including approaches relying heavily on visual input.
  • Listening comprehension of passages: the researchers did not assess how well both groups understood the listening passages used during instruction.
  • Further research: larger samples of VI learners and interventions to develop L2 listening proficiency are needed.

Speaker notes · 1.5 minutes

These limitations matter when applying the findings. Because AIM alone was not tested, the researchers cannot say whether the increased volume, the L1 explanation, or their combination accounts for the observed advantage. The authors also call for more research on listening proficiency development in VI learners and on how learners themselves use strategies to understand enhanced aural input.

14 · Implications for university English teachers

What might teachers take into practice?

Make key words noticeable

Use deliberate aural cues when introducing target vocabulary in listening activities.

Use L1 strategically

Where appropriate, use concise L1 explanations to support L1–L2 meaning connections.

Build listening proficiency

Do not assume auditory access automatically means high L2 listening proficiency.

Plan for retention

Revisit words in meaningful contexts and include interactive, productive use after initial explanation.

Practical caution: These are teaching implications informed by the article, not a tested university-level intervention. Adapt them to learners’ needs and evaluate their effects locally.

Speaker notes · 2 minutes

Make a clear distinction between the study and your professional recommendations. The study involved Saudi female high-school learners at low–intermediate proficiency. University teachers can consider adapting the principles, but should not assume identical effects for adult university learners, different proficiency levels or other languages. Also, increased volume should be used carefully and accessibly; the article tested increased volume as a cue, not excessively loud sound.

15 · Discussion

Questions for our teaching context

Think–pair–share

  1. How do we currently draw learners’ attention to important vocabulary in listening tasks?
  2. When can L1 explanations support learning, and when might they create over-reliance on translation?
  3. How can we provide repeated, meaningful and productive encounters with target words?
  4. How might these approaches be adapted for VI learners in inclusive university classes?

Speaker notes · 2 minutes

Invite colleagues to discuss one or two questions. Encourage them to connect the findings to their own courses, while remembering the study’s population and limitations. For VI learners, ask whether spoken cues, accessible documents, screen-reader compatibility and opportunities for oral practice are built into the activity from the beginning.

16 · Conclusion

The central message

AIMCS—enhanced volume followed by an L1 explanation—improved short-term vocabulary learning compared with CS alone for both VI and sighted learners in this study.

  • VI learners showed greater overall short-term gains than SI learners.
  • Listening proficiency was particularly important for VI learners.
  • The AIMCS advantage did not persist at the two-week delayed post-test.
  • Longer-term retention may require further contextualized encounters and productive language use.

The wider contribution: evidence for an aural, inclusive approach to vocabulary instruction—while recognizing that more research is needed.

Speaker notes · 1 minute

Close by returning to the title: “bridging barriers” means designing instruction that makes language input accessible and noticeable, while supporting meaning. The strongest message is not that one technique solves every challenge, but that carefully designed aural input and strategic L1 support can improve short-term vocabulary learning, especially for VI learners, and should be followed by work that supports durable learning.
